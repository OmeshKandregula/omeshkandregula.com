The silence was the signal

Closed beta, 53 users, March to June 2026

For two months I thought the beta was going well. Nobody wrote to me with problems. A few people said nice things. If you had asked me how it was going I would have said good, and the only thing I could have pointed at was that nobody was complaining.

So I stopped waiting for feedback and went looking for it.

01

I watched instead of asking.

I installed session recording and watched people use the site. The first thing I saw was somebody scroll straight past the part I was proudest of, a long example showing how one story lands differently for a founder, a student, and a researcher. He never finished onboarding. Nothing was broken, so there was nothing for him to email me about.

02

I picked who to interview using data, not availability.

I ranked users by how often they opened the brief against how many they had been sent, kept only people who had received at least 50, and interviewed the top three. The people most likely to have an opinion worth hearing.

03

I had to ask the same question five ways.

Even the most engaged readers did not complain. Eventually one of them said the line explaining how a story affected them felt forced. Small annoyances never become complaints. They build up quietly, and then one day people stop opening the email.

Most things that happen in the world do not affect a specific person. My system tried to find a connection anyway, because I had built it to always find one. And it always did.

What I changed, and why

Four decisions from the beta. The first one removed the feature I had been proudest of.

01

The personalization line now stays quiet.

37% of notes declined, on purpose

The "For You" note explaining why a story matters to you now appears only when a story genuinely affects you. Over the last two weeks it declined to write one 37% of the time, on purpose. I also track that rate: Too low means the system stopped declining, too high means it stopped writing. The feature that made the product worth building was the same feature that was quietly driving people away, and the fix was to make it speak less.

539 of 1,474 notes · 14 days

02

I stopped using publishers' sentences.

Every story description is now written from scratch rather than lifted from the article. This is a legal exposure most newsletters ignore, and the case law is not friendly. It is enforced mechanically, not by good intentions: A generated line is rejected if it shares a seven word run with the source, and rejected again if it contains a number or a name that does not appear in the original. That second check is what stops the model from inventing facts while avoiding plagiarism.

03

Two versions of one story is worse than one fewer story.

Readers were getting the same news three times in a row under different headlines. The obvious fix, comparing stories by meaning using embeddings, did not work, because embeddings measure wording and newsrooms covering the same event deliberately choose different words. What survives paraphrase is the cast: The same people, places, and numbers appear in every version. So stories are grouped by the rare entities they share. The top events get the space, the rest become a short "also today" list, so a topic still covers the day without repeating itself.

04

No quality change ships on instinct.

Anything that changes how stories are scored gets run against real archived news first, and compared against the current behavior before it goes live.

System Architecture

Three stages, each an internal gate

1. Ingest & Cache

Nightly cron scans 496 active feeds. Quality filters keep only high-signal articles.

2. Enrich & Personalize

Role-specific "For You" insights per subscriber. Pre-rendered, zero AI calls at send time.

3. Deliver at 7 AM

Checks every 30 minutes for local 7 AM. Pre-built briefs ship via email.

The rebuild

Bulletyn started as a hackathon build on Supabase and a Lovable-generated React front end. That got it working. It was not going to survive a real product, so I rebuilt it on AWS.

Next.js on AWSInfrastructure as code with SST
Aurora Serverless v2With Drizzle, scaling to zero between runs
Step FunctionsNightly pipeline, stage by stage
Amazon SESDelivery, with bounce and complaint handling
AWS BedrockClaude Haiku, analysis and personalization

The pipeline runs overnight and pre-renders every brief before anyone wakes up. There are zero AI calls at send time, which is why a brief arrives at exactly 7 AM local rather than whenever a model finishes thinking.

Building a system that cannot hide

The beta taught me that silence hides problems. So I built the product to be incapable of it.

A degraded brief still sends.

So the health check read zero problems while readers quietly got worse briefs for five nights running. There is now an alarm on the degradation rate itself, not just on whether the email went out.

A failed AI call used to leave nothing behind.

No row, no error, just an absence. The table looked clean. Failures are now recorded as records, so a gap is something you can query instead of something you have to notice.

A broken story grouper looks exactly like a quiet news day.

Both produce one story per event. So the system measures something the news cannot move: How much substance it can extract from a headline. If that number falls, the code is broken, regardless of what happened in the world.

→The same lesson as the beta, one level down. Absence of a complaint is not evidence of health.

What has not worked yet

13 plays, ever, by 3 people

I shipped spoken briefs, an audio version of each morning's edition. It has been played 13 times, by 3 people.

I am not going to call that traction. The most likely reason is that it sits behind a click into a dashboard instead of arriving in the app people already open, which is a distribution problem, not an audio problem. The next test is putting it into a podcast feed rather than building more of it.
